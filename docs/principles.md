# Principles

1. **People and agents work as a pair, and swap roles.** The agent brings speed and breadth; the person brings context, judgment and accountability. Neither works alone at any stage. Every so often the person does the work, for example writing the code, and the agent reviews it. That keeps the person's skills sharp, and the agent records the squad's style in its context from what the person produces.
2. **The opportunity, the design and the acceptance criteria come first.** An agent's output is only as good as the opportunity statement, shared design, acceptance criteria and business context it is given. Effort here is cheaper than effort spent fixing the solution. As with traditional SDLCs, breaking down the requirements into the smallest possible iterations allows predictable delivery and faster flow to production.
3. **Verification is independent from creation.** Agents produce work faster, so checking has to keep up. Whoever or whatever made the work is never the only one to test it, and a second model or person reviews it with the aim of finding how it fails. Security and quality are checked throughout the flow, not at the end, by human and agent pairs or by agentic tools, with independent and human-led verification on top, such as regular human-led penetration testing. Checks are automated so they are repeatable, and security testing stays within an agreed scope and only against systems you own.
4. **People hold defined gates.** Spec approval, design decisions, merge and release are decided by people. Agents advise, and the accountable person decides.
5. **A squad owns the solution.** A squad is a small cross-functional team, with product, engineering and platform in it. The squad owns the opportunity and the solution together, and designs it jointly, in the room. Product brings the customer, the outcome and the real limits on scale, cost and time; engineering brings what is feasible and what it costs to build and run. Together they keep the design proportionate, neither over-built nor under-built. No single role designs it alone.
6. **Context lives where agents can read it, and someone owns it.** Conventions, architecture decisions, standards and knowledge of the business live in places agents can read, such as the repository, a shared domain context, or the brief. Each has an owner who keeps it current, because an agent working from stale context produces confident wrong answers.
